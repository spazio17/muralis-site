Privacy policy

This is the same text the app itself shows on its About screen, published here so it is readable without a device in front of you.

Muralis is a kiosk launcher that displays a web dashboard you choose, on a device you own. This policy says what the app does with data, in plain words, and is written against the EU General Data Protection Regulation, the GDPR.

What the app sends to the developer

Nothing. Muralis has no analytics, crash reporting or advertising identifiers of its own, and no server of its own; the billing library's diagnostic log, described below, goes to Google. The developer receives no data from your device, at any time, for any reason.

What the app sends elsewhere, and where

Nothing beyond your local network, until you configure a destination yourself, apart from the diagnostic log of Google Play's billing library described below. On your local network, a panel in a fleet, or one showing its Join code, announces itself so your other Muralis panels can find it. Two optional features publish data, and both stay off until you set them up: MQTT stays off until you enter a broker address, and the local web administration stays off until you set a password. Other features you set up reach only the addresses you give them, when they run: the network cameras you add, whose pictures are fetched only while a camera is shown and are never stored on the device, a pop-up's picture, page or camera stream, an automation that calls an address or plays a sound from one, Ambient's web page, and a short request to the dashboard's server every few seconds while the page is shown, to notice when it stops answering and when it comes back. "Say a sentence" hands its text to the speech engine installed on the device, under that engine's own terms.

The announcement on your network.

A panel in a fleet, or one showing its Join code, announces itself on your local network with Android's own service discovery (mDNS), the way printers and speakers do: its device identifier and name, its address on your network, the port other panels use to reach it, a fingerprint of its public key, and whether it is in a fleet or showing its Join code, with the first two digits of that code so the panel a code was typed for is the only one asked; the code is never sent, not even to that panel. When a panel of the fleet does not answer, the others look for it among these announcements by its key fingerprint. A panel in no fleet with its Join code closed announces nothing. The announcement does not leave your local network and carries nothing about you.

Google Play's billing library.

Muralis sells Pro through Google Play's billing library, which Google makes and which every copy of the app carries, Free included. The library keeps a diagnostic log of its own work and sends it to Google about once an hour, also on a device without Google Play or a Google account: the library's version, the app's name, the device's maker, model and Android build, the country and language, the mobile carrier and network type, and the outcome of each call to Google Play, such as "billing service unavailable". Google uses it to improve the library, under Google's privacy policy; the developer receives none of it. It carries no settings, addresses, pictures or anything the panel shows.

Joining a fleet.

When this panel joins a fleet, or another panel is added from it, the two panels exchange their device identifiers, their keys and the fleet's name and identifier over an encrypted connection on your network. A panel of a fleet with Muralis Pro can also show an invitation QR for a new tablet: it carries the address Muralis is downloaded from, a one-time code, and that panel's identifier, network address, member port and key fingerprint, and when the invitation ends; it works for ten minutes and for one tablet only; the tablet set up with it opens its Join code with that code and asks the inviting panel, at that address, to add it. When the panel added is still at its welcome screen, the panel adding it also gives it its corner-tap combinations and, if one is set, its PIN as a salted hash, never the PIN itself, so both panels have the same way out. While they are in a fleet, the panels tell each other every few minutes how they are: the app and Android versions, whether the panel is the device owner, its battery and whether it is charging, its Wi-Fi signal strength, how long the app has run, the page error it shows if any, and its clock; the Fleet page and the alerts use it. Every panel of the fleet then keeps the fleet's list of its panels, signed by the panel that last changed it: each panel's identifier, the name you gave it, its key, and the network address and port where the others reach it. If the fleet has Muralis Pro, the list also says which panel last confirmed it with Google Play and until when it holds: a panel's identifier and two dates, never your Google account or your purchase. When you share a group of settings with the fleet (the dashboard, MQTT, the web admin, the display or Ambient), those settings travel between your panels over the same encrypted connection, passwords included, and each panel stores them as its own settings; in the stored copy of the fleet's list the passwords are blanked. Each panel also keeps the last twenty versions of that list. A picture of a panel's screen is fetched by another panel of the same fleet only when a person asks for it on the Fleet page, over the fleet's own encrypted connection, and is not stored. None of it leaves your network, with one exception you set up yourself: if you share alerts with the fleet and enter an MQTT topic or a web address for them, a panel sends each alert there, to your broker or to that address, with the fleet's name, the panel's name and identifier, the kind of alert (offline, page error, battery, temperature, combination used, PIN lockout), whether it started or ended, a short detail such as the battery percentage, and the time, nothing else.

MQTT.

If you enter a broker address, Muralis publishes a status snapshot to that broker, about once a minute and sooner when something changes. It contains device status and diagnostics: battery, memory, storage, network state, the device's own address on your network, processor load and temperatures, screen state, the panel's settings including the device identifier it generates for itself, the app version, the health of the dashboard: which page is loaded, whether it loaded, how often the view crashed or was rebuilt, and, if Ambient shows your pictures, the name of the playlist in use and of the picture on screen. A picture of the screen itself goes to the broker only when you ask for one, with the screenshot command or the Home Assistant button. The picture is retained on the broker until you clear it there.

Three things in it deserve to be called out, because you might not expect them. The snapshot includes the address of the dashboard being shown, so if that address is itself something you would not want on your broker, do not enable MQTT. It includes the network address of any device that was locked out of the web administration for repeated wrong passwords, published so that you can act on a guessing attempt. And it is retained: your broker keeps the most recent copy and hands it to anything that subscribes later, even after the panel is switched off or removed, until you clear it on the broker.

The complete, current content of the snapshot is not a secret and cannot go stale in this document: it is visible to you at any time on your own broker, and in the web administration.

Local web administration.

If you set an administration password, the panel serves the same status information, plus its settings, over your local network to anyone who supplies that password. This surface also shows things that are deliberately never sent to the broker: the corner-tap combinations that unlock the kiosk, and the panel's connection settings. If Ambient shows your pictures, it also lists the picture folders on the panel, shows small previews and the pictures themselves, and lets whoever has the password upload pictures to the panel and delete the ones that were uploaded. Whoever has the password can also upload a certificate and its private key for the panel to serve instead of its own, and delete them again. Whoever has the password can also fetch a picture of the screen as the panel shows it, and a diagnostics file: the app version, the device's make, model, Android and browser engine versions, whether the app is the device owner, the permissions it holds, the same status information, a few counters (how often the view crashed or was rebuilt, lockouts of the web administration, the last page and its last error) and the last lines of the app's own log. No password, key or join code is in it, and every web address in it is cut to its host and path. Both are made when asked for and sent only to the browser that asked; the app keeps neither and uploads nothing anywhere. The password is what protects the way out of the kiosk; treat it accordingly.

What it is all for. The snapshot exists so that your own dashboard can show the panel's state and control it, and the web administration exists so that you can change the panel's settings without walking to the wall. Nothing in either is used for analytics, advertising, profiling or any purpose beyond making the app work.

MQTT is not encrypted unless you switch on Encrypted (TLS) for a broker that offers it: without it, the snapshot and your broker credentials travel in clear text over your network, so use it on a network you trust. The web administration is served over HTTPS with a certificate the panel makes for itself, so your browser warns about it once, or with a certificate of your own that you upload; the panel's settings screen shows the fingerprint of the certificate in use for you to compare, and a request over plain HTTP is answered with a redirect to the encrypted address. A certificate and its key can only be uploaded over HTTPS. If the panel cannot make a certificate it serves plain HTTP and its settings screen says so.

The Wi-Fi network name is never read or transmitted. Reading it would require the location permission, which the app holds only for the Bluetooth beacons sensor, because Android counts a Bluetooth scan as a use of location: on an ordinary install Android asks you when you tap Allow on that sensor, and a panel set up as device owner grants it to itself at start, with the other sensors' permissions, so their switches work with nobody at the panel. The app reads neither the network name nor a position with it.

Who is responsible for that data

You are. The broker and the network are yours: the developer is not a recipient of anything the app publishes and cannot read, correct or delete any of it. The developer processes personal data in exactly one case: if you write to the contact address below, he receives your address and what you wrote, uses them to answer you, and nothing else. For that mail the developer is what the GDPR calls the controller, and the legal basis is that you asked (Article 6(1)(b) and (f) of the GDPR).

What the app stores on the device

Your settings, a device identifier Muralis generates itself, and the corner-tap combinations you record. If the panel is in a fleet, also its own fleet key, kept encrypted like the credentials below, and the fleet's signed list described above with its last twenty versions: the fleet's name and identifier, each panel's identifier, name, key, network address and port, the groups, the settings shared with the fleet with their passwords blanked, and the keys of panels removed from it. Your broker credentials and, if you upload one, a client certificate for the broker, the administration password, the page sign-in if you set one (given only to your dashboard's own address when it asks), the user and password of each camera you add (sent only to that camera), the Pro purchase record, the private key of the panel's own HTTPS certificate and, if you upload one, your certificate and its private key are stored encrypted through the Android keystore; the uploaded certificate's names, end date and fingerprint, which the certificate itself makes public, are stored with your settings, and so are the hosts and fingerprints of the page certificates you chose to trust. A settings file you export from the web administration holds your settings, including the corner-tap combinations and every address as you wrote it (with any name and password written inside an address), and, only if you ask for them, your passwords and PIN, which are then encrypted with a passphrase you choose; it goes only to the browser that asked. A setup QR made there carries the same settings without passwords or PIN, and nothing in it is encrypted, so keep it like a key. The dashboard view stores whatever cookies and sessions your dashboard sets, in the same way a browser would. If you allow the page to use the camera or the microphone, Muralis passes them to the page when it asks, as a browser would, and the page decides where its pictures and sound go; both are off until you switch them on. To notice a dashboard that has frozen, the app compares two size measurements of the page against their previous values, on the device; they are never stored beyond that comparison and never transmitted.

If Ambient shows your pictures, the app also stores the playlists you make, the names you give pictures for their credit line, the pictures you upload, and small previews of the pictures you browsed. Your own pictures are read where they are and never copied, changed or deleted; the one thing Muralis deletes is an uploaded copy, when you ask it to.

Muralis reads the photos on the device only if you have Ambient show the panel's own pictures, and only after you grant the permission Android asks for at that moment, or, on a panel set up as device owner, after it grants itself that permission at start, as it does the sensors' permissions; it reads them to list the folders, draw the previews and show the pictures you picked, and for nothing else. For App lock, which shows one installed app instead of a web page, Muralis reads the list of apps on the device that have an icon to open, to let you choose one, shows that list on the panel and in the web admin, and sends the name of the chosen app in the status snapshot; with your permission to display over other apps it lays four small invisible squares in the corners over that app, which take the taps landing there for your escape combinations, and the status light's frame. It does not read your contacts, messages or call history, and requests no permission that would allow it to.

Sensors

Every sensor that reads the room (the camera, the microphone, Bluetooth beacons, NFC, light, proximity, movement and the other hardware sensors) is off until you switch it on on the app's Sensors page; the panel's own readings (battery, power, network, memory, processor, display and Ambient) are on, as they always were. When a sensor is on, its reading is part of the status message and is shown in the web admin, and the app's own automations can react to it on the device. The camera is also opened, whatever its switch says, while Create a fleet or Add a panel in the Fleet section reads another panel's join code; those pictures are read on the device and neither kept nor sent. The camera, when on, streams live pictures to the web admin behind its password and, only if you switch that on too, sends a picture to your broker when it sees movement or when you ask for one; that picture is retained on the broker until the switch or the camera goes off, or you clear it there; nothing is recorded. The microphone, when on, reports how loud the room is as a number from 0 to 100; no sound is recorded or sent. Separately, if you allow it in the page options, the dashboard page itself, and only a page on the dashboard's own address, may use the camera and the microphone as a website does in a browser; what that page does with them is up to the page and whoever runs it, not Muralis. Bluetooth, when on, listens for beacons near the panel and never sends one; each beacon in reach goes into the status message with its identifier, the name you gave it, its signal strength and estimated distance. The scan needs the location permission on every Android version, because Android counts a Bluetooth scan as a use of location; the app reads no position with it. NFC, when on, reads a tag held to the panel: its identifier and any text or web address written on it, both part of the status message, and the identifier is also sent to your broker on its own topic as a tag read. The names you give beacons are stored on the device like your other settings, as are the identifiers of the last twenty tags read and when each was read, so the automations can offer them. The app's own log, which the web admin shows and lets you copy or download for a support mail, carries what the app does: tag identifiers, the names of your automations, the addresses of sounds it was asked to play without their secrets, page errors and the network addresses locked out of the web admin.

Deletion

Uninstalling the app, or clearing its data, removes everything it has stored, including the encrypted credentials, the uploads and the previews. The copies outside its reach are the retained status snapshot on your broker and, if you had pictures to the broker on, the camera's last picture and the last picture of the screen, all of which you clear there.

If you buy Muralis Pro

The purchase is handled by Google Play. Google is the seller, takes the payment and issues the receipt. The developer never sees your name, your address or your payment details, and receives no report of who bought anything.

To know whether Muralis Pro is on your account, Muralis asks the Google Play app on the same device what the signed-in account has bought, and stores Google's signed answer, encrypted like the other credentials, so the panel keeps it with no network and no account. Whether Google Play then talks to Google's own servers is Google's business, covered by Google's privacy policy rather than this one. On a device without Google Play there is no one to ask; the billing library still keeps and sends the diagnostic log described under What the app sends elsewhere.

Children

Muralis is not directed at children. It has no accounts, no profiles, no messages and nothing anyone can post, and it asks for nothing about the person using it. What it can send is the state of the device, described above, and it goes to your broker and your network, never to the developer.

Your rights

The GDPR gives you rights over personal data someone holds about you: access, correction, deletion, restriction, portability and objection. They run against whoever holds the data. For everything this app handles, that is you, on your own device and your own services, and the DELETION section above is how you exercise them there. The developer holds nothing beyond any mail you send him, which he answers and will delete on request. You also have the right to complain to a data protection authority; for the developer that is the Austrian one, dsb.gv.at.
